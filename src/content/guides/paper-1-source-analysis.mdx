## The paper at a glance

Paper 1 lasts **one hour** (plus five minutes' reading time) and is based on the prescribed subject your class has studied. You receive four sources — usually a mix of written documents, images, cartoons or statistics — and answer four questions worth **24 marks** in total. It is worth 30% of the grade at Standard Level and 20% at Higher Level.

| Question | Marks | What it asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1(a) | 3 | What does a source say? Pick out three relevant points. |
| 1(b) | 2 | What is the message of a visual source? |
| 2 | 4 | Analyse the **value and limitations** of one source, with reference to its origin, purpose and content. |
| 3 | 6 | **Compare and contrast** what two sources say about an issue. |
| 4 | 9 | Using the sources **and your own knowledge**, answer an essay-style question. |

## Questions 1(a) and 1(b): comprehension

Keep these short. For 1(a), make three clear, separate points, each clearly drawn from the source. For 1(b), state the message of the image and point to the details that show it — the symbols, captions, labels and figures the artist chose.

## Question 2: origin, purpose, content

Value and limitations come from **who** produced the source, **when**, **for whom** and **why**. A strong answer ties every claim to a specific feature of the source.

- **Origin:** Who created it, when, and in what position? A government minister in 1938 knows what the cabinet decided but has reasons to present it favourably.
- **Purpose:** Why was it made? A speech to parliament persuades; a private diary records; a propaganda poster mobilises.
- **Content:** What does it contain, and how? Loaded language, selective statistics or a one-sided view limit it; detail and specificity add value.

<Callout type="warning">
"It is biased, so it is limited" earns little credit. Bias can be valuable: a propaganda poster is excellent evidence of how a regime wanted to be seen. Explain *what* the source is valuable *for*.
</Callout>

## Question 3: compare and contrast

Aim for both **similarities and differences**, linked point by point rather than describing one source and then the other. Quote or refer precisely to both sources in each point. Several well-developed links beat a long list of thin ones.

## Question 4: the mini-essay

This is worth the most marks, so leave time for it — around 25 minutes. Write a short argument that answers the question directly, uses **all four sources** as evidence, and adds **your own knowledge** of the topic. Group sources by argument rather than going through them one by one.

## Timing

A common plan: about 5 minutes for question 1, 10 for question 2, 12 for question 3 and 25–30 for question 4.
