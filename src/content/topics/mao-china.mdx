## Overview

On 1 October 1949 Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China in Beijing. The Chinese Communist Party (CCP) had survived near-destruction in the 1930s, grown during the war against Japan, and defeated Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists (Guomindang) in a civil war. Over the next quarter of a century Mao led China through campaigns of extraordinary ambition and violence, until his death in 1976.

## Why did the Communists win?

- **War with Japan.** The Guomindang bore the brunt of Japan's attacks and was weakened; the CCP expanded behind Japanese lines and won a reputation as patriotic resisters.
- **Peasant support.** Land reform in Communist areas and disciplined troops won support in the countryside.
- **Guomindang weaknesses.** Corruption, hyperinflation and repression cost Chiang the support of the cities and the middle class; many of his troops defected.
- **Leadership and strategy.** Mao's guerrilla strategy and generals such as Lin Biao, and captured Japanese and American weapons, made the People's Liberation Army formidable by 1948.

## Consolidating power

In the early 1950s land reform redistributed land and saw hundreds of thousands, perhaps millions, of landlords killed at mass "struggle sessions". Campaigns against counter-revolutionaries and corruption targeted opponents and businessmen. The Korean War allowed Mao to present the regime as the defender of China against American imperialism.

In 1956 Mao invited criticism: <Q by="Mao Zedong" date="1956" source="Launching the Hundred Flowers Campaign" note="Whether the campaign was a genuine invitation or a trap to expose critics is debated — but the Anti-Rightist Campaign that followed silenced intellectuals for a generation.">Let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred schools of thought contend</Q>. When the criticism proved fierce, the Anti-Rightist Campaign branded some 550,000 people "rightists".

## The Great Leap Forward

Mao aimed to overtake Britain's industrial output within fifteen years by mobilising China's population. Peasants were organised into huge <Term id="peoples-communes">people's communes</Term>; millions made steel in backyard furnaces; and exaggerated harvest reports led the state to requisition grain that did not exist. The result was the worst famine in history. When Marshal Peng Dehuai criticised the policy at Lushan in 1959, he was purged, and the campaign continued.

## The Cultural Revolution

Sidelined after the famine, Mao launched the Cultural Revolution in 1966 to purge the party of those he accused of following a "capitalist road" — above all President Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping — and to revive revolutionary zeal. Millions of students joined the <Term id="red-guards">Red Guards</Term>, waving the <Term id="little-red-book">Little Red Book</Term> and attacking teachers, officials and the "Four Olds". The chaos led Mao to send the army in and millions of young people to the countryside. The cult of Mao reached its height, while the country's education system and cultural heritage suffered enormous damage.

<Callout type="exam" title="Comparing Mao and Stalin">
Both used terror, propaganda and a cult of personality; both pursued rapid industrialisation at the expense of the peasantry. But Mao relied far more on mass mobilisation — and in the Cultural Revolution turned the masses against his own party.
</Callout>

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Maurice Meisner" work="Mao's China and After" year="1977; 3rd ed. 1999" school="Modernisation">
Meisner, while critical of Mao's methods, stressed that China was transformed into an industrial power with improved literacy and life expectancy.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Jung Chang and Jon Halliday" work="Mao: The Unknown Story" year="2005" school="Hostile biography">
Chang and Halliday portrayed Mao as a monster responsible for more peacetime deaths than any other leader. Many scholars criticised their selective use of sources, even while accepting the scale of the suffering.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Frank Dikötter" work="Mao's Great Famine" year="2010" school="Archival history">
Using provincial party archives, Dikötter estimated at least 45 million premature deaths in 1958–62, many caused by violence as well as starvation — a figure at the high end of estimates.
</Perspective>

<Recall q="What were Mao's aims in launching the Cultural Revolution?">
To remove rivals such as Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping, to reassert his own authority after the failure of the Great Leap Forward, and to revive revolutionary zeal among the young.
</Recall>
