## Overview

In 1519 Hernán Cortés landed on the Gulf coast of Mexico with about 500 men. By August 1521 the Mexica (Aztec) capital, <Term id="tenochtitlan">Tenochtitlan</Term>, lay in ruins and its last ruler, Cuauhtémoc, was a prisoner. The conquest was not simply a Spanish achievement: it depended on Indigenous allies who saw the Spaniards as a means to overthrow Mexica domination — and on an epidemic that devastated the city.

## The Mexica Empire in 1519

The Mexica ruled a tributary empire from their island capital in Lake Texcoco. Tenochtitlan had perhaps 200,000 inhabitants, larger than any city in Spain. Conquered peoples paid heavy tribute and supplied victims for human sacrifice, and powerful enemies — above all Tlaxcala — remained unconquered. Those resentments were the conquistadors' greatest asset.

## Why did the conquest succeed?

**Indigenous allies.** Tlaxcala and other altepetl (city-states) provided tens of thousands of warriors, porters and supplies. For them this was a war against the Mexica, in which the Spaniards were useful allies. The final siege was fought largely by Indigenous armies.

**Disease.** Smallpox, to which the population had no immunity, killed a large part of Tenochtitlan's population in 1520, including Cuitláhuac, just as the Mexica had driven the Spaniards out.

**Translation and intelligence.** <Term id="la-malinche">Malintzin</Term>, a Nahua woman given to the Spaniards, interpreted and advised Cortés, allowing him to negotiate and exploit political divisions.

**Technology and tactics.** Steel weapons, armour, horses, crossbows and a few cannon gave the Spaniards advantages in close combat; brigantines built for the siege controlled the lake. Their importance is easy to overstate: guns were few and gunpowder scarce.

**Different ways of war.** Mesoamerican warfare often aimed to capture enemies for sacrifice rather than kill them; the Spaniards fought to destroy.

<Callout type="warning" title="A myth to avoid">
The story that Moctezuma believed Cortés was the returning god Quetzalcoatl comes from sources written after the conquest. Many historians now treat it as a later invention that helped explain defeat — do not present it as fact.
</Callout>

## Impact

- **Population collapse.** Repeated epidemics and exploitation reduced the Indigenous population of central Mexico dramatically over the sixteenth century.
- **Colonial rule.** Tenochtitlan was rebuilt as Mexico City, capital of New Spain. Spaniards were granted Indigenous labour and tribute through the encomienda, while Indigenous nobles often kept local authority under Spanish rule.
- **Religion.** Franciscan and other missionaries led mass conversions, destroying temples and codices, but also recording Nahua culture — as in Bernardino de Sahagún's Florentine Codex.
- **Debate in Spain.** Friars such as Bartolomé de las Casas condemned the conquistadors' cruelty, leading to the New Laws of 1542 and the Valladolid debate of 1550–51.

The Nahua perspective survives in accounts recorded after the conquest. One lament begins:

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="William H. Prescott" work="History of the Conquest of Mexico" year="1843" school="Traditional narrative">
Prescott told the conquest as the heroic achievement of Cortés and a small band of Spaniards — a view that shaped popular understanding for more than a century.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Ross Hassig" work="Mexico and the Spanish Conquest" year="1994" school="Indigenous politics">
Hassig argued that the conquest must be understood through Mesoamerican politics: the Spaniards succeeded because they became part of an Indigenous war against Mexica dominance.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Matthew Restall" work="Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest" year="2003" school="Revisionist">
Restall challenged myths such as the "handful of adventurers", the idea that the conquest was complete in 1521, and the belief that Indigenous people saw the Spaniards as gods.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why were the Tlaxcalans willing to fight alongside the Spaniards?">
Tlaxcala was an independent enemy of the Mexica, surrounded by Mexica territory and at constant war with them. After initially fighting the Spaniards, its leaders saw an alliance as the best way to defeat their old enemy.
</Recall>

<Recall q="How did smallpox affect the course of the conquest?">
It struck Tenochtitlan in 1520, soon after the Spaniards had been driven out, killing much of the population and the ruler Cuitláhuac. It weakened the city's defence before the siege of 1521.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What did the New Laws of 1542 try to do, and why did they fail?">
They tried to protect Indigenous people by banning their enslavement and phasing out the encomienda. Settlers in the Americas resisted fiercely — in Peru they rebelled — and the Crown withdrew the most controversial provisions.
</Recall>
