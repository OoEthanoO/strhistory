## Overview

The <Term id="enabling-act">Enabling Act</Term> passed on 23 March 1933 and was published the next day. It allowed the Reich government to make laws without parliamentary approval, including laws departing from the constitution.

<Chronology>
  <Event date="23 March 1933">The Reichstag passed the act in the Kroll Opera House.</Event>
  <Event date="24 March 1933">The law was published.</Event>
</Chronology>

The [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-enabling-act) reproduces the law and documents the intimidation surrounding its passage.

## Law and coercion

The Nazi leadership used persecution and intimidation to secure the outcome. Communist deputies and some Social Democrats were prevented from taking their seats. The vote cannot be assessed as if every elected representative participated freely.

<KeyQuote by="Reich government" date="24 March 1933" source="Enabling Act, Article 2 (extract); US Holocaust Memorial Museum translation of Reichsgesetzblatt I (1933), p. 141" analysis="The article continued with formal protections for parliamentary institutions and presidential rights. The quoted permission nevertheless authorised departures from other constitutional provisions.">Laws enacted by the Reich Government may deviate from the Constitution</KeyQuote>

## Interpreting the evidence

This source illustrates why legality and democratic legitimacy are different questions. The existence of a published law does not establish free consent. Conversely, evidence of coercion does not make the text irrelevant: the wording reveals the powers that the regime sought to exercise.

For a thematic argument about authoritarian rule, connect a precise institutional change to its consequences. A claim that a leader simply became powerful leaves the mechanism unexplained.
