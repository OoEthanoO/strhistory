## Overview

In 1948 the Afrikaner-led <Term id="national-party">National Party</Term> won power in South Africa promising <Term id="apartheid">apartheid</Term> — "separateness". Over the next decade it built a system of laws that classified every South African by race and controlled where they could live, work, study and travel. Resistance grew from mass nonviolent campaigns to sabotage, until the state banned the liberation movements and imprisoned their leaders. The <Term id="rivonia-trial">Rivonia Trial</Term> of 1963–64 marks the end of this prescribed subject.

## Building apartheid

Segregation existed long before 1948, but apartheid made it systematic. The Population Registration Act classified everyone by race; the Group Areas Act assigned residential areas by race, leading to forced removals such as District Six and Sophiatown; marriages and sexual relations across racial lines were banned; the pass laws controlled Black Africans' movement; and the Bantu Education Act gave Black children an education designed to prepare them for manual labour. Under Hendrik Verwoerd (Prime Minister from 1958) "grand apartheid" aimed to divide Black South Africans into separate ethnic "homelands".

## Resistance

**Nonviolent mass action, 1949–1959.** The ANC Youth League — Nelson Mandela, Walter Sisulu and Oliver Tambo among them — pushed the ANC towards boycotts, strikes and civil disobedience. The Defiance Campaign of 1952 saw thousands deliberately break apartheid laws. In 1955 the Congress Alliance adopted the Freedom Charter.

<KeyQuote by="The Congress of the People" date="26 June 1955" source="The Freedom Charter, Kliptown" analysis="The Charter's non-racial vision — South Africa for all its peoples — defined the ANC's aims. Africanists who rejected cooperation with white, Indian and coloured allies broke away to form the PAC in 1959.">South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white, and that no government can justly claim authority unless it is based on the will of all the people.</KeyQuote>

**Sharpeville and its aftermath.** On 21 March 1960 police opened fire on a PAC demonstration against the pass laws at Sharpeville, killing 69 people. The government declared a state of emergency and banned the ANC and PAC. South Africa became a republic in 1961 and left the Commonwealth. Weeks before Sharpeville, the British Prime Minister had warned the South African parliament of the changes sweeping Africa:

<KeyQuote by="Harold Macmillan" role="British Prime Minister" date="3 February 1960" source="Speech to the South African Parliament, Cape Town" analysis="Macmillan signalled that Britain would not support white minority rule against African nationalism — a sign of South Africa's growing international isolation.">The wind of change is blowing through this continent, and, whether we like it or not, this growth of national consciousness is a political fact.</KeyQuote>

**Armed struggle.** With peaceful protest banned, Mandela and others formed Umkhonto we Sizwe ("Spear of the Nation") in 1961, beginning a sabotage campaign against infrastructure while trying to avoid loss of life. The PAC's armed wing, Poqo, was prepared to kill. The state responded with detention without trial and torture. At the Rivonia Trial, Mandela ended his statement from the dock:

<KeyQuote by="Nelson Mandela" role="Accused No. 1" date="20 April 1964" source="Statement from the dock at the Rivonia Trial" analysis="Mandela used the trial to explain to the world why the ANC had turned to armed struggle. The speech made him an international symbol of resistance — even as the verdict crushed the movement inside South Africa for a decade.">I have cherished the ideal of a democratic and free society in which all persons live together in harmony and with equal opportunities. It is an ideal which I hope to live for and to achieve. But if needs be, it is an ideal for which I am prepared to die.</KeyQuote>

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Liberal historians" work="e.g. Leonard Thompson, A History of South Africa" year="1990" school="Race and Afrikaner nationalism">
Liberal historians explained apartheid mainly through Afrikaner nationalism and racial ideology, which they saw as at odds with economic growth.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Harold Wolpe" work="'Capitalism and cheap labour-power in South Africa'" year="1972" school="Radical / Marxist">
Radical historians argued that apartheid served capitalism by guaranteeing a supply of cheap, controlled Black labour.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Deborah Posel" work="The Making of Apartheid, 1948–1961" year="1991" school="Contested policy">
Posel showed that apartheid was not a single master plan but was shaped by conflicts within the state and between different white interests.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Name three apartheid laws and what they did.">
The Population Registration Act (1950) classified everyone by race; the Group Areas Act (1950) assigned separate residential areas, enabling forced removals; the Bantu Education Act (1953) gave Black Africans an inferior, state-controlled education. Others include the Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act and the pass laws.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why was Sharpeville a turning point?">
The killing of 69 protesters brought international condemnation, a state of emergency and the banning of the ANC and PAC. With legal protest impossible, the movements turned to armed struggle.
</Recall>

<Recall q="How did the ANC justify turning to violence in 1961?">
Decades of nonviolent protest had brought only harsher repression, and the movement had been banned. Umkhonto we Sizwe targeted infrastructure through sabotage, aiming to avoid loss of life while pressuring the government.
</Recall>
