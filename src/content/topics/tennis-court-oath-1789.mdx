## Overview

On 20 June 1789, deputies gathered in an indoor tennis court at Versailles and pledged to continue meeting until a constitution had been established. They had already declared themselves a National Assembly on 17 June. The event challenged who could speak for France.

<Chronology>
  <Event date="17 June 1789">Deputies declared a National Assembly.</Event>
  <Event date="20 June 1789">The assembly took the Tennis Court Oath.</Event>
</Chronology>

The [Palace of Versailles account](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/key-dates/jeu-paume-oath-1789) supplies the sequence and the translation below.

## Why did representation matter?

The <Term id="estates-general">Estates-General</Term> represented three orders with different privileges. The National Assembly's claim to represent the nation challenged this division. Its determination to continue meeting also challenged the monarch's control over political deliberation.

<KeyQuote by="Deputies of the National Assembly" date="20 June 1789" source="Tennis Court Oath; English translation published by the Palace of Versailles" analysis="The pledge made continued collective action the means of securing a constitution. It declared an intention, not proof that a constitution already existed.">We swear never to separate and to meet wherever circumstances require until the kingdom’s Constitution is established and grounded on solid foundations.</KeyQuote>

## Interpreting the evidence

A useful distinction is between a declaration of authority and the ability to enforce it. The oath revealed resolve; further evidence is needed to explain the balance of power outside the meeting room. The [Bastille note](/topics/storming-bastille-1789) follows a different group of actors in Paris.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="William Doyle" work="The French Revolution: A Very Short Introduction" year="2001">
Doyle placed the Revolution's legacy in changes to government, public life and ideas about rights. That wider frame invites inquiry into what made a single political pledge consequential.
</Perspective>

See [the publisher's account of Doyle's book](https://academic.oup.com/book/655). The event-level question is how far a new claim to authority changed institutions in practice.

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why was the oath more than a promise to hold another meeting?">
The deputies asserted that their duty to establish a constitution justified continuing as a national assembly. This challenged royal control over the assembly's existence and authority.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why does the oath alone not prove that France became a democracy in June 1789?">
It stated an intention to establish a constitution. It did not itself settle the franchise, create completed institutions or demonstrate equal political participation.
</Recall>
