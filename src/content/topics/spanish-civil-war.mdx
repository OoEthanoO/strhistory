## Overview

In July 1936 army officers rose against Spain's elected <Term id="popular-front">Popular Front</Term> government. The coup failed in the big cities but succeeded across much of the country, and Spain split into two zones. For almost three years Franco's <Term id="spanish-nationalists">Nationalists</Term>, backed by Germany and Italy, fought the Republic, backed by the USSR and the <Term id="international-brigades">International Brigades</Term>. The war ended in April 1939 with a Nationalist victory and a dictatorship that lasted until Franco's death in 1975.

## Causes

**Long-term divisions.** Spain was split between landowners and landless labourers in the south, between a powerful Catholic Church and anticlerical workers, between a centralising state and Catalan and Basque nationalists, and over the army's place in politics.

**The Second Republic's reforms** — land reform, secular education, army reform and Catalan autonomy — went too far for the right and not far enough for many on the left. Politics polarised: a centre-right government (1933–35) reversed reforms, the Asturias rising was brutally crushed, and the Popular Front's victory in 1936 was followed by strikes, land seizures and political killings.

**The trigger.** The murder of the monarchist leader José Calvo Sotelo on 13 July 1936 convinced waverers to join the conspiracy of generals that had already been planned.

## The war and foreign intervention

The Nationalists' crucial early advantage was Franco's Army of Africa — Spain's most effective troops — which German and Italian aircraft carried across the Strait of Gibraltar. Britain and France led a policy of non-intervention, fearing a wider war, but Germany, Italy and the USSR ignored it.

| Nationalists received | The Republic received |
| --- | --- |
| Germany's Condor Legion (aircraft, tanks, advisers) | Soviet tanks, aircraft and advisers — paid for with Spain's gold reserves |
| Tens of thousands of Italian troops | International Brigades of volunteers from dozens of countries |
| Supplies from Portugal; oil on credit from US companies | Limited help from Mexico |

When Madrid was threatened, the communist deputy Dolores Ibárruri rallied its defenders with the slogan <Q by="Dolores Ibárruri ('La Pasionaria')" date="July 1936" source="Radio broadcast" note="The slogan became the rallying cry of the Republic's defence of Madrid — and of anti-fascists across Europe.">¡No pasarán! — They shall not pass!</Q>

## Why did the Nationalists win?

- **Unity.** Franco combined military and political command, merging the right-wing parties into one movement in 1937. The Republic was divided between liberals, socialists, communists and anarchists, who fought over whether to make a revolution or win the war first.
- **Foreign aid.** German and Italian help was more consistent and effective than Soviet aid, and non-intervention hurt the legal government more than the rebels.
- **Military professionalism.** The Nationalists had most of the army's officers and its best troops.
- **Economic and international factors.** The Nationalists held the main food-producing areas for much of the war and found credit abroad more easily.

## Significance

The war was seen as a battle between fascism and democracy — or between order and revolution. Germany and Italy tested weapons and tactics, including terror bombing; the Rome–Berlin Axis was strengthened; and Britain and France's non-intervention looked to many like another form of appeasement. Both sides committed mass killings; Nationalist repression continued long after 1939.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Hugh Thomas" work="The Spanish Civil War" year="1961" school="Classic narrative">
Thomas's comprehensive account set the war in its international context and remains the starting point for its political and military history.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Paul Preston" work="The Spanish Holocaust" year="2012" school="Repression and responsibility">
Preston emphasises the right's responsibility for the war and the systematic, planned character of Nationalist repression, which he distinguishes from the often uncontrolled killings in the Republican zone.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Stanley G. Payne" work="The Spanish Civil War" year="2012" school="Collapse of the Republic">
Payne places more weight on the radicalisation and political violence of the left in 1934–36, which he argues destabilised the Republic before the rising.
</Perspective>
