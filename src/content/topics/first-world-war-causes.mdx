## Overview

On 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb linked to the Serbian nationalist <Term id="black-hand">Black Hand</Term>, shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, in Sarajevo. Five weeks later the great powers of Europe were at war. Explaining how a regional crisis became a continental war means looking at both long-term tensions and the decisions made during the <Term id="july-crisis">July Crisis</Term>.

## Long-term causes

**Alliances.** Two armed blocs faced each other. The alliances were defensive on paper, but they meant a conflict involving one great power was likely to draw in others — and they gave Germany a fear of "encirclement" by France and Russia.

**Militarism and war plans.** Every power had detailed mobilisation plans built around railway timetables. Germany's <Term id="schlieffen-plan">Schlieffen Plan</Term> required it to attack France through Belgium as soon as Russia mobilised — turning any Russian–German crisis into a war in the West too.

**Imperial and naval rivalry.** Germany's <Term id="weltpolitik">Weltpolitik</Term> and its battle fleet pushed Britain towards France and Russia. Colonial crises over Morocco hardened attitudes.

**Nationalism in the Balkans.** Serbian nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary, a multinational empire with millions of South Slavs. After the Balkan Wars doubled Serbia's size, many in Vienna saw war with Serbia as a matter of survival.

<Callout type="exam">
"Long-term causes made war likely; the July Crisis made it happen." That is a useful starting point, but strong essays test it — would war have come without the specific decisions taken in Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg in July 1914?
</Callout>

## The July Crisis

Austria-Hungary decided to use the assassination to crush Serbia. It first sought German backing — and received the "blank cheque" of unconditional support. The ultimatum of 23 July was designed to be unacceptable; Serbia's conciliatory reply was rejected anyway. Russia, protector of Serbia and unwilling to be humiliated as in 1908, mobilised. Germany's war plan now dictated the next steps: war on Russia, then France, and the invasion of neutral Belgium.

Britain's entry was not automatic. The German invasion of Belgium — whose neutrality Britain had guaranteed in 1839 — gave the British government its cause. ## Historians' debate

The debate began with the peace treaty itself. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles — the <Term id="war-guilt-clause">war guilt clause</Term> — made Germany and its allies accept responsibility for the war.

<Perspective historian="Fritz Fischer" work="Griff nach der Weltmacht (Germany's Aims in the First World War)" year="1961" school="German responsibility">
Fischer argued that Germany's leaders deliberately risked — even sought — a war to achieve European dominance, pointing to ambitious war aims drawn up in September 1914. His work caused a storm in West Germany.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="A. J. P. Taylor" work="War by Time-table" year="1969" school="Mobilisation plans">
Taylor stressed how rigid railway mobilisation plans took decisions out of statesmen's hands once mobilisation began.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Christopher Clark" work="The Sleepwalkers" year="2012" school="Shared responsibility">
Clark argued that responsibility was shared among all the powers, whose leaders — "sleepwalkers", watchful but unseeing — made a series of decisions without grasping where they led. Critics say he underplays German and Austrian choices.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why did the Schlieffen Plan make a wider war more likely?">
It assumed a two-front war and required Germany to attack France quickly through Belgium as soon as Russia mobilised. Any crisis with Russia therefore became a war with France, and the invasion of Belgium brought in Britain.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What was the 'blank cheque', and why does it matter?">
Germany's promise of unconditional support to Austria-Hungary on 5–6 July 1914. It encouraged Vienna to take a hard line against Serbia, knowing Germany would back it even if Russia intervened.
</Recall>

<Recall q="How does Clark's interpretation differ from Fischer's?">
Fischer placed the main responsibility on Germany's leaders, who he argued pursued aggressive aims. Clark spread responsibility among all the powers, emphasising misjudgement and the complexity of the crisis rather than a deliberate drive to war.
</Recall>
