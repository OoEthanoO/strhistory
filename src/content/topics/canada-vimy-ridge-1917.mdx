## What happened?

- For the first time, all four divisions of the Canadian Corps attacked together, side by side, under the British general Sir Julian Byng
- The goal for the Canadian Corps was to seize Vimy Ridge, a highly fortified position, as part of a larger British offensive at Arras
- Vimy Ridge was one of the most formidable defensive positions on the Western Front, with complex tunnels and trenches defended by machine guns and artillery
- French assaults on the ridge in 1914 and 1915 had been repulsed with heavy <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term>
- In order to succeed this time, the Canadian Corps made extensive preparations for the upcoming offensive, including
  1. Digging tunnels for secure command posts, medical stations and communications, and to bring troops up to the front line under cover
  2. Scale models of the terrain and full-scale practice fields, so that officers and soldiers knew exactly where to go
  3. Using aerial photographs to print more than 40,000 maps, so that even sergeants and section commanders knew the ground
- The Canadians were helped by recent technological developments, which included
  - Aircraft that photographed German positions and directed artillery fire, at such a cost to British airmen that April 1917 became known as “Bloody April”
  - <Term id="tank">Tanks</Term>, although the few available achieved little in the mud
  - The portable Lewis gun, which transformed the machine gun from a stationary defensive weapon into a mobile offensive one that protected troops on the attack
- Before the battle, the German defenders were shelled for almost three weeks, ending in *Trommelfeuer* or drumfire, artillery fire so dense and loud it resembled a drumbeat; German soldiers called the final week “the week of suffering”
- During the battle, Canadian artillerymen perfected the creeping barrage, in which artillery would constantly fire just ahead of the advancing infantry to provide continuous artillery cover
- Among the commanders was Arthur Currie of the 1st Division, who in June 1917 became the first Canadian to command the Canadian Corps
- When the attack began at 5:30 a.m. on Easter Monday, 9 April 1917, the extensive preparations led to a decisive Canadian victory: by early afternoon three of the four divisions had taken their objectives, which two years of earlier offensives had failed to achieve
- On the evening of 9 April, the 85th Battalion (Nova Scotia Highlanders), in a feat of extraordinary daring, charged Hill 145, the ridge’s highest point, without any artillery support; the hill was secured the next day, and the last German position, “the Pimple”, fell on 12 April
- Despite the victory, 9 April 1917 was the bloodiest day in Canadian military history; the whole battle cost 10,602 Canadian <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term>, including 3,598 killed

## Why is it important?

- The victory at Vimy Ridge became and continues to be a defining moment of national pride for Canadian identity
- Fighting together with their fellow countrymen for the first time, the four Canadian divisions had shown the world what Canadian unity, ingenuity and bravery could achieve
- After months of slaughter on the Somme, the Canadians had revolutionized their tactics and planning to fit a fully modern war, showing the extraordinary adaptability of Canadian military minds
- The victory at Vimy strengthened the argument that Canadian soldiers, and the Canadian nation, were not inferior to the British
- The success of the Canadian Corps in this battle cemented their reputation as elite, dependable shock troops who could be counted on to overwhelm even the most resolute defences
- Vimy is often described as Canada’s coming of age, when the glory of this victory became inseparable from an emerging Canadian consciousness
  - Historians such as Tim Cook (*Vimy: The Battle and the Legend*, 2017) have shown that much of this meaning grew after the war, especially once the Vimy Memorial was unveiled in 1936
- That Canadians had stood, fought and prevailed against one of the strongest German positions on the Western Front showed that Canada deserved a place in the world reflecting its growing military strength
