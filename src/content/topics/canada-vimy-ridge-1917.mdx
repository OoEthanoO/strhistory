## Overview

After months of reorganisation following the Somme, the four divisions of the <Term id="canadian-corps">Canadian Corps</Term> were allowed to fight together as a unified force for the first time, rather than as detachment units attached to British formations. Their objective was Vimy Ridge — a highly fortified escarpment that had repulsed Allied assaults in 1914 and 1915 with heavy casualties and no success. As part of the larger Allied Arras offensive, the Canadians were given the task no other force had managed to complete. They succeeded.

## Preparations: the key to Canadian success

The Canadian approach to Vimy Ridge was defined by meticulous preparation — a direct response to the unplanned catastrophes of the Somme. Under the command of General Sir Julian Byng, with Arthur Currie commanding the 1st Division (soon to be promoted to full commander of the <Term id="canadian-corps">Canadian Corps</Term>), the Canadians made unprecedented preparations:

1. **Secure command infrastructure.** Tunnels were dug for command posts, medical stations and communication lines, protecting them from German artillery.
2. **Scale models and rehearsals.** Full-scale replicas of the terrain were built so that junior officers and NCOs could walk the ground they would attack, knowing exactly where to lead their men.
3. **40,000 maps.** Aerial reconnaissance produced detailed maps distributed down to the platoon level — every soldier knew his objective.

## Technological advantages

The Canadian forces also benefited from recent technological developments:

- **Air superiority.** Aerial <Term id="dogfighting">dogfights</Term> were conducted to win control of the skies over Vimy, preventing German aircraft from observing the preparations.
- **Tanks** were deployed in larger numbers than at the Somme.
- **The Lewis Gun** — a portable, lighter machine gun — transformed the role of the machine gun from a stationary defensive weapon into a mobile offensive one, protecting infantry during the advance.
- **The <Term id="creeping-barrage">creeping barrage</Term>.** Canadian artillerymen perfected this technique, in which artillery fire moved forward in precisely timed lifts just ahead of the advancing infantry, keeping the enemy suppressed until the last moment.

Before the assault, the German Army endured 13 days of *Trommelfeuer* (drumfire) — artillery fire so dense and continuous it resembled a drumbeat.

## The assault

At 5:30 a.m. on Easter Monday, 9 April 1917, all four Canadian divisions attacked simultaneously along a 6.4-km front. The meticulous preparation paid off: three of four objectives were achieved within two hours — something that two years of Allied offensives had failed to accomplish.

<Callout type="context">
The Nova Scotia Highlanders performed one of the battle's most remarkable feats: they seized Hill 145, the highest point of the ridge and the most heavily defended, without any artillery support — a charge of extraordinary daring that secured the ridge's commanding position.
</Callout>

Despite the victory, the battle was the bloodiest day in Canadian military history to that point: 3,598 killed and approximately 7,000 wounded. The price of success was immense, but the cost of failure — as earlier Allied commanders had found — would have been higher still.

## Significance: a 'coming of age' moment

The battle's significance extended far beyond its tactical results.

**Military reputation.** The success at Vimy cemented the Canadian Corps' reputation as elite shock troops — the force Allied commanders called upon when a position had to be taken at all costs.

**Evidence against colonial inferiority.** For two years, British commanders had tended to treat Canadian troops as colonial supplements to British formations. Vimy, where Canadians fought alone and succeeded where others had failed, solidified the argument that Canadian soldiers and the Canadian nation were not inferior to their British counterparts.

**Canadian unity.** For the first time, men from every province of Canada — Quebecers alongside Nova Scotians, Albertans alongside Ontarians — fought together as a single national army. The victory was inseparable from a sense of what Canada could achieve when united.

**National identity.** Vimy Ridge became and remains a defining moment of Canadian national pride. The victory demonstrated Canadian ingenuity, adaptability and bravery on the world stage — qualities that became central to how Canadians understood themselves.

<Callout type="exam">
The contrast between the Somme and Vimy Ridge is a productive area for analysis. Both involved Canadian troops in major Western Front battles within twelve months of each other. The difference in outcomes — catastrophic at the Somme, decisive at Vimy — illustrates the importance of preparation, tactical learning, and unified command.
</Callout>

As Arthur Currie reflected afterward, the Canadian Corps had revolutionised its tactics between the Somme and Vimy, fitting a modern war of industrial firepower with new methods: decentralised initiative, coordinated artillery and infantry, and thorough intelligence. Canada had shown the extraordinary adaptability of its military mind.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Pierre Berton" work="Vimy" year="1986" school="Popular/nationalist history">
Berton argues that Vimy Ridge was the moment Canada truly became a nation — that the shared sacrifice and shared victory of all four Canadian divisions created a national consciousness that transcended regional and linguistic divisions. In his account, Vimy is the emotional birth certificate of modern Canada.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Jack Granatstein" work="Canada's Army: Waging War and Keeping the Peace" year="2002" school="Military history">
Granatstein situates Vimy within a longer story of Canadian military professionalism, arguing that the victory was the product of institutional learning — the Canadian Corps applying the lessons of the Somme systematically and creatively. The national myth, he suggests, is not wrong, but it rests on real military achievement that deserves analysis on its own terms.
</Perspective>
