## Overview

Kosovo, a province of Serbia with an Albanian majority, lost its autonomy in 1989 under Slobodan Milošević. A decade of peaceful Albanian resistance was followed by the rise of the <Term id="kla">Kosovo Liberation Army</Term> and a brutal Serbian counter-insurgency. In March 1999 NATO began a 78-day bombing campaign — <Term id="operation-allied-force">Operation Allied Force</Term> — without the approval of the UN Security Council. Serbian forces withdrew in June, and Kosovo came under UN administration.

## Causes

**Competing claims.** For Serbs, Kosovo was the heartland of medieval Serbia and the site of the Battle of Kosovo (1389), central to Serbian national identity. By the 1980s about 90% of its population was Albanian, and Albanians demanded full republic status.

**Milošević and Serbian nationalism.** Milošević won power by championing Kosovo's Serbs. At Kosovo Polje in 1987 he told Serbs who had clashed with police that <Q by="Slobodan Milošević" date="24 April 1987" source="Remark to Serb protesters at Kosovo Polje" note="Translations vary — often rendered 'No one should dare to beat you'. The televised moment made Milošević the champion of Serbian nationalism and launched his rise.">No one should be allowed to beat you</Q>. In 1989 Kosovo's autonomy was abolished, and Albanians were dismissed from state jobs, schools and hospitals.

**The failure of nonviolence.** Rugova's Democratic League of Kosovo built a parallel state with its own schools and health care. But when the Dayton Accords of 1995 left Kosovo unmentioned, many Albanians concluded that only violence would win international attention — and the KLA grew rapidly, helped by weapons looted from Albania's arsenals in 1997.

## War and intervention

Serbian security forces responded to KLA attacks with operations that destroyed villages and killed civilians — including the Jashari family compound in Prekaz (1998) and, in January 1999, 45 Albanians at Račak. After the Rambouillet talks failed, NATO began bombing Yugoslavia on 24 March 1999.

Western leaders justified the campaign as a humanitarian necessity:

<KeyQuote by="Tony Blair" role="British Prime Minister" date="22 April 1999" source="Speech to the Economic Club of Chicago ('Doctrine of the International Community')" analysis="Blair argued that the principle of non-interference in other states' affairs must be qualified when governments commit atrocities — a key statement of the case for humanitarian intervention.">This is a just war, based not on any territorial ambitions but on values.</KeyQuote>

During the bombing, Serbian forces drove some 800,000 Albanians out of Kosovo — an act of <Term id="ethnic-cleansing">ethnic cleansing</Term>. NATO's strikes also caused civilian deaths, including in the mistaken bombing of the Chinese embassy in Belgrade. Milošević accepted NATO's terms in June; Russia's diplomatic support for a settlement helped.

## Was it legal? Was it legitimate?

Russia and China opposed the intervention, and there was no Security Council resolution authorising force. An independent commission concluded that the intervention was <Q by="Independent International Commission on Kosovo" date="2000" source="The Kosovo Report" note="The phrase captures the dilemma of humanitarian intervention — acting to stop atrocities without the authority of international law. It fed into the later 'Responsibility to Protect' doctrine.">illegal but legitimate</Q>.

## Aftermath

- Kosovo was administered by the UN (UNMIK) and secured by NATO-led KFOR; many Serbs and Roma fled revenge attacks.
- Milošević lost power in October 2000, was extradited to The Hague in 2001, and died in 2006 before a verdict.
- Kosovo declared independence in 2008; Serbia and several other states do not recognise it.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Noel Malcolm" work="Kosovo: A Short History" year="1998" school="Historical claims">
Malcolm challenged Serbian nationalist myths about Kosovo's history, including the idea that Albanians were recent arrivals in a Serbian heartland.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Tim Judah" work="Kosovo: War and Revenge" year="2000" school="Journalistic history">
Judah traced how both Serbian repression and KLA strategy — including provoking Serbian overreaction to win Western support — drove the conflict.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Noam Chomsky" work="The New Military Humanism" year="1999" school="Critic of intervention">
Chomsky argued that NATO acted from strategic self-interest rather than humanitarian motives, and that the bombing escalated the atrocities it claimed to prevent.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why did many Kosovo Albanians turn from Rugova's nonviolence to the KLA?">
Years of peaceful resistance had not restored autonomy, and the Dayton Accords of 1995 ignored Kosovo — suggesting that the international community only responded to violence. Weapons from Albania's collapse in 1997 also became available.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why was NATO's intervention controversial in international law?">
It had no UN Security Council authorisation, because Russia and China would have vetoed it. Supporters argued it was justified to prevent a humanitarian catastrophe; critics saw it as an illegal use of force against a sovereign state.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What did Resolution 1244 establish?">
It placed Kosovo under a UN interim administration (UNMIK), authorised an international security presence (KFOR), and required the withdrawal of Yugoslav forces — while formally reaffirming Yugoslavia's sovereignty.
</Recall>
