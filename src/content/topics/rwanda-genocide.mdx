## Overview

Between April and July 1994, around 800,000 people — most of them Tutsi, along with Hutu who opposed the killing — were murdered in Rwanda in about 100 days. The genocide was organised by extremists within the Hutu-dominated government, carried out by the army, militias such as the <Term id="interahamwe">Interahamwe</Term> and ordinary civilians, and ended only when the <Term id="rwandan-patriotic-front">Rwandan Patriotic Front</Term> won the civil war. A small UN force, <Term id="unamir">UNAMIR</Term>, was present throughout but was not allowed to stop it.

## Causes

**Colonial rule.** German and then Belgian rulers treated Hutu and Tutsi — groups that shared language, religion and culture — as separate races. The Belgians ruled through a Tutsi elite and in the 1930s issued identity cards recording ethnicity, hardening the categories that the génocidaires would use.

**Independence and exclusion.** The "Hutu revolution" of 1959–62 overturned Tutsi dominance; violence drove tens of thousands of Tutsi into exile, and those who stayed faced discrimination and periodic massacres.

**Civil war and economic crisis.** The RPF, formed by Tutsi exiles in Uganda, invaded in 1990. A collapse in coffee prices and structural adjustment deepened poverty. Extremists around President Habyarimana's circle — the *akazu* — spread <Term id="hutu-power">Hutu Power</Term> ideology and portrayed all Tutsi as RPF collaborators.

**Preparation.** Militias were trained and armed, lists of targets were drawn up, and the radio station <Term id="rtlm">RTLM</Term> broadcast propaganda that called Tutsi *inyenzi* ("cockroaches").

## The genocide

Within hours of the plane crash, the Presidential Guard and militias began killing Tutsi and moderate Hutu, including the Prime Minister, Agathe Uwilingiyimana, and the ten Belgian peacekeepers guarding her. Roadblocks checked identity cards. Local officials organised the killing, and neighbours killed neighbours. Churches, schools and stadiums where people had sought refuge became massacre sites.

## International failure

<Callout type="context" title="Why didn't the world act?">
The failure of the US-led intervention in Somalia (1993) made Washington reluctant to send troops to Africa; the Security Council — the USA especially — avoided calling the killing "genocide", which would have raised obligations under the 1948 Genocide Convention; Belgium withdrew its troops after its peacekeepers were killed; and France had close ties to the Habyarimana regime.
</Callout>

<KeyQuote by="Independent Inquiry into the actions of the United Nations during the 1994 genocide in Rwanda" date="December 1999" source="Report commissioned by the UN Secretary-General" analysis="The UN's own inquiry assigned responsibility to the whole system — the Secretariat, the Security Council and member states. Compare this with explanations that blame individual states or officials.">The failure by the United Nations to prevent, and subsequently, to stop the genocide in Rwanda was a failure by the United Nations system as a whole.</KeyQuote>

In Kigali in 1998 President Clinton acknowledged that the international community <Q by="Bill Clinton" date="25 March 1998" source="Speech at Kigali airport" note="An admission of failure — but critics note that the US had actively worked to limit the UN's response in 1994.">did not act quickly enough after the killing began</Q>.

## Aftermath

- The RPF government under Paul Kagame took power; revenge killings by RPF soldiers also occurred.
- Génocidaires regrouped in refugee camps in Zaire, contributing to the Congo wars from 1996.
- The ICTR in Arusha convicted leading organisers; its Akayesu judgment (1998) was the first to recognise rape as an act of genocide.
- The failure in Rwanda shaped later debates on <Term id="humanitarian-intervention">humanitarian intervention</Term> — including over Kosovo in 1999.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Gérard Prunier" work="The Rwanda Crisis: History of a Genocide" year="1995" school="Political elite">
Prunier emphasised the role of the ruling elite, who organised the genocide to hold on to power as the civil war and Arusha threatened them.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Mahmood Mamdani" work="When Victims Become Killers" year="2001" school="Colonial legacy">
Mamdani argued that colonial rule turned Hutu and Tutsi into political, racialised identities — "natives" and "settlers" — which made genocide thinkable.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Scott Straus" work="The Order of Genocide" year="2006" school="Perpetrators">
Interviewing hundreds of convicted perpetrators, Straus found that wartime fear, local pressure and state authority mattered more than long-held ethnic hatred or radio propaganda alone.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="How did Belgian colonial rule contribute to the genocide?">
The Belgians treated Hutu and Tutsi as distinct races, favoured a Tutsi elite and introduced identity cards recording ethnicity. This hardened the division and gave the génocidaires a way to identify victims.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why was UNAMIR unable to stop the killing?">
It was small, lightly armed and had a peacekeeping mandate that did not allow it to use force to protect civilians. When its commander warned of genocide plans in January 1994 he was refused permission to act, and in April the Security Council cut the force to a few hundred troops.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why did the USA avoid calling the killing 'genocide'?">
Using the word could have created a legal and moral obligation to act under the Genocide Convention. After Somalia, the Clinton administration did not want to commit troops to Africa.
</Recall>
