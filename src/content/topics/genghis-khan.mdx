## Overview

Temüjin was born around 1162 into a minor Mongol clan. After his father was poisoned, his family was abandoned by their followers and survived in poverty. Through alliances, betrayals and war he united the peoples of the steppe, and in 1206 a <Term id="kurultai">kurultai</Term> proclaimed him Genghis Khan. By his death in 1227 his armies had conquered northern China's Jin frontier, destroyed the <Term id="khwarazmian-empire">Khwarazmian Empire</Term> and raided as far as the Caucasus and southern Russia.

## Rise to power

Temüjin's rise combined personal loyalty and ruthless politics. He built a following of companions (*nökör*) bound to him personally rather than by kinship, allied with the Kereit leader Toghril, and then turned on rivals — including his childhood friend Jamukha — and on the Tatars, Kereits and Naimans. Defeated tribes were broken up and redistributed among his units, weakening old loyalties to clan and lineage.

<Callout type="context" title="Sources">
The main Mongol source, the *Secret History of the Mongols* (13th century), was written for the Mongol elite and celebrates Temüjin's rise. Persian historians such as Juvaini and Rashid al-Din wrote in the service of later Mongol rulers; Chinese and Russian chroniclers wrote as victims. Each perspective shapes what it records.
</Callout>

## The Mongol war machine

- **Organisation.** The army was organised on a <Term id="mongol-decimal-system">decimal system</Term>, with units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 commanded by men chosen for loyalty and ability. An elite guard, the <Term id="keshig">keshig</Term>, protected the khan and trained future commanders.
- **Mobility.** Every warrior had several horses, allowing armies to cover great distances and to outmanoeuvre their enemies.
- **Tactics.** Mounted archers, feigned retreats and encirclement defeated larger armies in open battle.
- **Siege warfare.** Captured Chinese and Muslim engineers gave the Mongols siege machines that made walled cities vulnerable.
- **Terror and intelligence.** Cities that resisted were often destroyed and their populations killed, encouraging others to surrender; merchants and scouts gathered intelligence in advance.
- **Law and discipline.** The <Term id="yassa">Yassa</Term>, Genghis Khan's code of laws and decrees, imposed discipline and punished desertion severely.

## The conquest of Khwarazm

When the governor of Otrar massacred a Mongol caravan and the shah executed a Mongol envoy, Genghis Khan invaded. The campaign of 1219–1221 destroyed the Khwarazmian Empire. At Bukhara, according to the Persian historian Juvaini, he addressed the city's notables from the pulpit of the mosque:

<KeyQuote by="Genghis Khan" role="as reported by Juvaini" date="1220" source="History of the World-Conqueror (written c. 1260)" analysis="Juvaini served the Mongols, yet he recorded the destruction of Muslim cities in vivid detail. The speech — possibly embellished — shows how conquest was justified as divine punishment. Cities such as Merv and Nishapur were devastated, irrigation systems were wrecked and huge numbers were killed. Numbers in medieval chronicles are often exaggerated, but the destruction in Khwarazm and northern Persia was real and long-lasting.

## Impact

- **Destruction.** Northern China and Persia suffered massive loss of life and economic damage.
- **Empire-building.** Genghis Khan created an administration that relied on literate subjects — Uighur scribes adapted their script for Mongolian — and a relay system of messengers.
- **Connections.** His successors' empire later linked China and Europe in the <Term id="pax-mongolica">Pax Mongolica</Term>, encouraging trade along the Silk Roads.
- **Religious policy.** The Mongols generally tolerated all religions and exempted clergy from taxation, so long as communities submitted.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="David Morgan" work="The Mongols" year="1986" school="Balanced assessment">
Morgan emphasised the scale of destruction in Persia and China while recognising the Mongols' administrative achievements and their reliance on the expertise of conquered peoples.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Jack Weatherford" work="Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World" year="2004" school="Positive reassessment">
Weatherford presented Genghis Khan as a builder of connections — of trade, communications and religious tolerance — whose empire helped create the modern world. Critics argue that his account underplays the human cost.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Timothy May" work="The Mongol Art of War" year="2007" school="Military history">
May explained Mongol success through organisation, training, logistics and adaptability rather than numbers or ferocity alone.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="How did Temüjin weaken tribal loyalties among his followers?">
He broke up defeated tribes and redistributed their people across new decimal units commanded by men loyal to him, and he rewarded personal loyalty and merit over kinship.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Name four reasons for the Mongol army's success.">
Decimal organisation and discipline; mobility from multiple horses per warrior; tactics such as mounted archery and feigned retreats; siege expertise from captured engineers; the use of terror to encourage surrender; and good intelligence.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why must historians treat Juvaini's account of Bukhara with care?">
Juvaini wrote around 1260, decades after the events, as an official in the service of the Mongols. His account may be embellished, and his numbers — like many medieval chroniclers' — may be exaggerated.
</Recall>
