## Overview

In November 1095, at the Council of Clermont, Pope Urban II called on the knights of Western Europe to help the Christians of the East and free Jerusalem from Muslim rule. The response astonished contemporaries. Within four years a Latin army had crossed Anatolia, taken Antioch after a gruelling siege, and captured Jerusalem in July 1099 — founding the <Term id="crusader-states">crusader states</Term> that would survive in some form for almost two centuries.

## Causes

**The Byzantine appeal.** After the Seljuk victory at Manzikert (1071) the Byzantine Empire lost most of Anatolia. Alexios I wanted Western mercenaries to help recover it — not a mass pilgrimage army with its own agenda.

**Papal ambition.** Urban II saw an opportunity to assert papal leadership of Christendom during the conflict with the German emperor, to heal the schism of 1054 with the Eastern Church, and to redirect knightly violence away from Europe — the "Peace of God" movement had already tried to limit it.

**Religious motivation.** Crusaders were offered spiritual rewards for an armed pilgrimage. The chronicler Fulcher of Chartres reported Urban's promise:

<KeyQuote by="Pope Urban II" role="as reported by Fulcher of Chartres" date="November 1095" source="Speech at the Council of Clermont" analysis="This is the promise of an indulgence — remission of the penalty for sins — for those who took the cross. It explains why nobles sold or mortgaged land to go: for many the crusade was an act of penance, not a business venture. Remember that Fulcher wrote after the crusade's success.">All who die by the way, whether by land or by sea, or in battle against the pagans, shall have immediate remission of sins.</KeyQuote>

The crowd's reported response, <Q by="The crowd at Clermont" source="as reported by Robert the Monk" note="The cry became the crusaders' battle slogan. Robert wrote around 1107, so his account is shaped by what the crusade became.">Deus vult! — God wills it!</Q>, captures the religious enthusiasm the call released. The <Term id="indulgence">indulgence</Term>, the prestige of pilgrimage to Jerusalem, and apocalyptic expectation all played a part.

**Material motives?** Older accounts stressed land-hungry younger sons. Historians now point out that crusading was ruinously expensive and that most crusaders who survived went home — which suggests material gain was secondary for most.

<Callout type="historiography">
Weigh the motives against each other rather than listing them. The strongest answers distinguish between the motives of the pope, the great lords (some, like Bohemond of Taranto, clearly wanted principalities), and ordinary knights and pilgrims.
</Callout>

## Why did it succeed?

- **Muslim division.** The Seljuk sultanate had fragmented after 1092, and the Sunni Seljuks and Shi'a Fatimids of Egypt were rivals. The Fatimids had themselves retaken Jerusalem from the Seljuks in 1098.
- **Byzantine support.** Guides, supplies and the recovery of Nicaea gave the expedition a foothold in Anatolia.
- **Western military strengths.** The charge of heavily armoured knights and the crusaders' ability to fight as a coherent force when it mattered — at Dorylaeum and against Kerbogha.
- **Religious conviction.** Belief in divine support sustained morale through starvation at Antioch; the "discovery" of the Holy Lance there gave the army renewed confidence.
- **Luck and timing.** Kerbogha's coalition fell apart in battle, and Egyptian reinforcements reached Jerusalem too late.

## Consequences

- Four crusader states were established: Edessa, Antioch, Jerusalem and (by 1109) Tripoli.
- Relations with Byzantium soured when Bohemond kept Antioch instead of returning it to Alexios.
- The Rhineland massacres began a pattern of anti-Jewish violence associated with crusading.
- In the Islamic world the immediate impact was limited, but the loss of Jerusalem later became a rallying cry for the <Term id="jihad">jihad</Term> of Zengi, Nur al-Din and Saladin, who retook the city in 1187.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Steven Runciman" work="A History of the Crusades" year="1951–54" school="Critical, pro-Byzantine">
Runciman portrayed the crusades as a tragic episode that damaged Byzantium and poisoned relations between Christians and Muslims, ending with a famous condemnation of the whole movement as an act of intolerance.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Jonathan Riley-Smith" work="The First Crusade and the Idea of Crusading" year="1986" school="Religious motivation">
Using charters that record how crusaders financed their journeys, Riley-Smith argued that most were driven by genuine piety and penitential zeal, not by the hope of land or loot.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Carole Hillenbrand" work="The Crusades: Islamic Perspectives" year="1999" school="Islamic perspectives">
Hillenbrand showed how Muslim writers experienced the crusades — at first as a local, Frankish problem — and how the idea of jihad against the Franks developed over the twelfth century.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why did Alexios I ask the West for help, and why was he disappointed by what arrived?">
He needed soldiers to recover Anatolia from the Seljuk Turks after Manzikert. He hoped for disciplined mercenaries under his command; instead came huge independent armies, including the disorderly People's Crusade, whose leaders pursued their own goals — Bohemond kept Antioch for himself.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Give three reasons the First Crusade succeeded.">
Divisions among the Muslim powers (the fragmented Seljuks and the rival Fatimids); Byzantine logistical support in Anatolia; and the crusaders' religious conviction and military cohesion at key battles such as Dorylaeum and against Kerbogha at Antioch.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why do historians doubt that most crusaders went for land and wealth?">
Crusading was extremely expensive — many sold or mortgaged property to go — and most survivors returned home rather than settling in the East. Charters show crusaders describing their motives in religious terms.
</Recall>
