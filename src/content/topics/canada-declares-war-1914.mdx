## Overview

On 4 August 1914, when Britain declared war on Germany following the invasion of Belgium, Canada was automatically brought into the First World War. As a <Term id="dominion">Dominion</Term> of the British Empire, Canada controlled its own internal affairs but had no independent <Term id="foreign-affairs">foreign affairs</Term>. <Term id="sovereignty">Sovereignty</Term> over relations with other nations remained in London. Canada's entry into the war was not a choice made in Ottawa — it was a consequence of empire.

<Chronology title="Key dates">
  <Event date="4 Aug 1914">Britain declares war on Germany following the invasion of Belgium; Canada, as a Dominion, is automatically at war.</Event>
  <Event date="5 Aug 1914">The Canadian Parliament meets and endorses the war, voting funds and committing to raise an expeditionary force.</Event>
  <Event date="Oct 1914">The first contingent of the Canadian Expeditionary Force — about 33,000 men — sails for Britain.</Event>

## Canada as a Dominion

In 1914 Canada occupied an unusual position within the British Empire. Under the terms established by the British North America Act of 1867 and subsequent conventions, Canada governed its own <Term id="domestic-affairs">domestic affairs</Term> — taxation, parliamentary representation, the state budget — but was represented solely by Britain on the international stage. When Britain declared war, every Dominion of the Crown was automatically at war alongside it.

This meant that Canada could not control its own interactions with other nations. It had no embassy, no treaty-making power of its own, and no ability to remain neutral when Britain chose to fight. Canadian soldiers would serve under British command structure, though Canada would insist on greater autonomy as the war progressed.

## Significance

<Callout type="context">
The 1914 declaration illustrated that Canadian sovereignty was still largely incomplete. The country had self-government at home but was not yet a fully equal actor on the world stage. The experience of the First World War — the sacrifices made and the battles won — would directly accelerate Canada's push for full independence, culminating in the Statute of Westminster (1931), which gave Dominions full legislative equality with Britain.
</Callout>

The event is significant for three reasons:

**Limited sovereignty.** Canada's inability to control its own entry into a foreign war showed that Dominion status was not equivalent to full national independence. Canada could not avoid the war even had it wished to.

**Representation without full equality.** Despite fighting alongside European nations as a military equal, Canada was still represented internationally by Britain rather than by its own diplomats. This contradiction would drive Canadian demands for independent status.

**The beginning of transformation.** Canada in August 1914 was a young country of eight million people, with a small professional military and an untested volunteer force. The four years that followed would fundamentally reshape Canadian national identity.

<Callout type="tip">
When analysing this event, distinguish between **domestic affairs** (where Canada was self-governing) and **foreign affairs** (where Britain spoke for the whole Empire). The First World War blurred this line, as Canada's military contribution demanded a greater voice in Allied decision-making.
</Callout>

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Robert Bothwell" work="Canada and Quebec: One Country, Two Histories" year="1998" school="Canadian nationalist">
Bothwell argues that Canada's automatic entry into the war, though constitutionally unavoidable, generated a political will within Canada to assert greater autonomy — making 1914 not just a beginning of sacrifice but the starting point of a long constitutional evolution toward independence.
</Perspective>
