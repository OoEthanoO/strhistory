## Overview

On 14 October 1962 an American <Term id="u-2">U-2</Term> spy plane photographed Soviet missile sites under construction in Cuba. For thirteen days President Kennedy and Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev confronted each other in the most dangerous crisis of the Cold War. It ended when Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for an American pledge not to invade Cuba — and a secret promise to remove US missiles from Turkey.

## Why did Khrushchev send the missiles?

- **To defend Cuba.** After the Bay of Pigs and continuing US efforts to overthrow Castro, Khrushchev feared an American invasion of the USSR's only ally in the Americas.
- **To redress the strategic balance.** The USA had far more long-range missiles than the USSR, and US Jupiter missiles in Turkey could reach Soviet cities. Missiles in Cuba were a cheap way to narrow the gap.
- **To gain leverage.** A stronger position might help over Berlin, where Khrushchev had repeatedly tried to force the Western powers out.
- **Prestige.** Khrushchev faced criticism at home and from China for weakness towards the West.

## The thirteen days

Kennedy's advisers in the ExComm debated air strikes and invasion. Kennedy chose a naval blockade — called a "quarantine", since a blockade is an act of war — which gave Khrushchev time to back down.

<KeyQuote by="John F. Kennedy" role="President of the United States" date="22 October 1962" source="Televised address to the nation" analysis="Kennedy made the danger public and set out a policy of deterrence in the starkest terms, while choosing a measured first step — the quarantine — that left room for negotiation.">It shall be the policy of this Nation to regard any nuclear missile launched from Cuba against any nation in the Western Hemisphere as an attack by the Soviet Union on the United States, requiring a full retaliatory response upon the Soviet Union.</KeyQuote>

When Soviet ships stopped at the quarantine line on 24 October, Secretary of State Dean Rusk remarked: <Q by="Dean Rusk" date="24 October 1962" source="Remark to a colleague during the crisis" note="The line captures the idea of brinkmanship — but the crisis was far from over; its most dangerous day was still to come.">We're eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked.</Q>

Khrushchev's letter of 26 October showed his fear of where the crisis was heading:

<KeyQuote by="Nikita Khrushchev" role="Soviet leader" date="26 October 1962" source="Letter to President Kennedy" analysis="The private letter reveals a leader looking for a way out. Its offer — missiles out for a no-invasion pledge — became the basis of the settlement, though a harder second letter the next day complicated matters.">We and you ought not now to pull on the ends of the rope in which you have tied the knot of war, because the more the two of us pull, the tighter that knot will be tied.</KeyQuote>

On 27 October a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, and a Soviet submarine under attack by US depth charges came close to firing a nuclear torpedo. That evening Robert Kennedy told the Soviet ambassador that the Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be removed if the deal remained secret. Khrushchev accepted the next morning.

## Consequences

- A **hotline** between Washington and Moscow (1963) and the **Partial Test Ban Treaty** (1963) began a period of cautious cooperation.
- Kennedy's reputation rose; Khrushchev's secret gain over Turkey could not be publicised, and he was removed from power in 1964.
- The USSR resolved never again to be so outmatched and launched a massive build-up of nuclear weapons, leading towards <Term id="mutually-assured-destruction">mutually assured destruction</Term>.
- Castro, excluded from the negotiations, felt betrayed by the USSR.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr." work="A Thousand Days" year="1965" school="Orthodox">
Schlesinger, a Kennedy aide, presented the crisis as a triumph of Kennedy's cool, measured leadership, which combined firmness with restraint.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Richard Ned Lebow and Janice Gross Stein" work="We All Lost the Cold War" year="1994" school="Revisionist">
Lebow and Stein argued that US threats against Cuba and the deployment of Jupiters in Turkey provoked the crisis, and that the outcome owed as much to Khrushchev's caution as to Kennedy's skill.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Aleksandr Fursenko and Timothy Naftali" work="One Hell of a Gamble" year="1997" school="Soviet archives">
Using Soviet sources, Fursenko and Naftali showed that the defence of Cuba was a central motive for Khrushchev, alongside the strategic balance.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why did Kennedy choose a 'quarantine' rather than an air strike?">
An air strike might not destroy every missile and would kill Soviet personnel, risking escalation to nuclear war. A quarantine showed resolve while giving Khrushchev time and a way to back down; calling it a quarantine avoided the language of a blockade, an act of war.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What did each side gain from the settlement?">
The USA had the missiles removed from Cuba. The USSR gained a US pledge not to invade Cuba and the secret removal of Jupiter missiles from Turkey — though it could not publicise the second gain.
</Recall>

<Recall q="How did the crisis change superpower relations?">
Both sides recognised the danger of nuclear war. They set up a hotline and signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty in 1963, beginning a period of cautious cooperation — but the USSR also began a major nuclear build-up.
</Recall>
