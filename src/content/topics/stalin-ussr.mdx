## Overview

When Lenin died in 1924, Joseph Stalin seemed an unlikely successor: a dull administrator beside the brilliant Trotsky. Within five years he had outmanoeuvred every rival. He then launched a "revolution from above" — the <Term id="five-year-plans">Five-Year Plans</Term> and forced <Term id="collectivisation">collectivisation</Term> — and consolidated his power through the <Term id="great-terror">Great Terror</Term> and a pervasive <Term id="cult-of-personality">cult of personality</Term>.

## The struggle for power

Stalin's post as General Secretary gave him control of party appointments, which he used to build a loyal network. He presented himself as a moderate, defended Lenin's legacy and backed <Term id="socialism-in-one-country">Socialism in One Country</Term> against Trotsky's "permanent revolution". His rivals underestimated him and failed to publish Lenin's criticism.

<KeyQuote by="Vladimir Lenin" role="Postscript to his 'Testament'" date="4 January 1923" source="Letter to the Party Congress" analysis="Lenin's warning could have ended Stalin's career. The party leadership agreed not to publish it — an early example of how Stalin's rivals, each fearing the others, helped him survive.">Stalin is too rude and this defect, although quite tolerable in our midst and in dealings among us Communists, becomes intolerable in a Secretary-General.</KeyQuote>

## Economic transformation

Stalin argued that the USSR must industrialise at breakneck speed to survive in a hostile world.

<KeyQuote by="Joseph Stalin" role="Speech to industrial managers" date="4 February 1931" source="'The Tasks of Business Executives'" analysis="The speech justified the extreme pace and human cost of the Five-Year Plans in terms of national survival. Ten years later Germany invaded — which Stalin's defenders used to vindicate him.">We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or we shall go under.</KeyQuote>

- **Industry.** Heavy industry, coal, steel and electricity grew dramatically, and new industrial cities such as Magnitogorsk were built. Targets were often unrealistic, figures inflated, and consumer goods neglected. Model workers were celebrated through the <Term id="stakhanovite-movement">Stakhanovite movement</Term>.
- **Agriculture.** Collectivisation forced peasants into collective farms to secure grain for the cities and exports. Resistance was met with deportation of "kulaks"; peasants slaughtered livestock rather than surrender it. The famine of 1932–33 killed millions.

## Terror and control

After Kirov's murder, repression widened into the Great Terror. Old Bolsheviks confessed to absurd charges in show trials; most of the Red Army's senior commanders were shot; and mass operations targeted "anti-Soviet elements" and national minorities. Around 700,000 people were executed in 1937–38 alone, and millions passed through the <Term id="gulag">Gulag</Term>.

Terror worked alongside propaganda: the cult of personality presented Stalin as the wise father of the nation, history was rewritten, and culture was bound to "socialist realism".

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Robert Conquest" work="The Great Terror" year="1968" school="Totalitarian model">
Conquest argued that the Terror was directed from the top by Stalin personally and caused enormous loss of life — a view largely confirmed on Stalin's role when Soviet archives opened.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="J. Arch Getty" work="Origins of the Great Purges" year="1985" school="Revisionist">
Getty argued that the purges grew partly out of chaos and conflict between the centre and regional officials, and that initiatives from below escalated repression.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Sheila Fitzpatrick" work="Everyday Stalinism" year="1999" school="Social history">
Fitzpatrick examined how ordinary people lived under Stalin — adapting, denouncing, and seeking advancement — to explain how the system functioned beyond the Kremlin.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="How did Stalin use the post of General Secretary to win power?">
It controlled appointments and promotions in the party. Stalin placed loyal supporters in key positions, who then voted for him at congresses and in the Central Committee.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What were the aims of collectivisation, and what were its consequences?">
To secure grain for the growing cities and for export, to finance industrialisation, and to bring the peasantry under state control. It caused the deportation of millions labelled kulaks, the slaughter of livestock, a collapse in production and the famine of 1932–33.
</Recall>

<Recall q="How do Conquest and Getty differ in explaining the Great Terror?">
Conquest stressed Stalin's personal direction of the Terror from above; Getty emphasised pressures, chaos and initiatives from lower levels of the party and state that escalated the repression.
</Recall>
