## What happened?

- In 1916, the German army launched a bold new strategy of <Term id="attrition">attrition</Term> against the French Army, with the goal of bleeding France white
- This strategy of <Term id="attrition">attrition</Term> boiled down to a singular point, the slaughterhouse of the Battle of Verdun in which the French army was almost broken
- In order to relieve pressure on their French allies, the British, under controversial commander Douglas Haig decided to launch an offensive to draw German forces away from Verdun
- This offensive took place at a region called the Somme, named after the Somme river which flowed through the battlefield
- For months, Allied but mostly British, Imperial and Canadian troops launched a series of catastrophic offensives against more defensible and elevated German positions

<figure>
  <img src="/g10-curriculum/Somme5.png" alt="Canadian troops making their way to the front" />

- The result was ‘Das Blutbad’ in German or the Bloodbath, in which whole <Term id="battalion">battalions</Term> were wiped out in minutes of action on the front
- On July 1st 1916, the first day of the offensive, 801 soldiers of the Newfoundland Regiment went <Term id="over-the-top">over the top</Term> to attack German trenches at Beaumont Hamel

<figure>
  <img src="/g10-curriculum/Somme6.png" alt="Canadian troops preparing to go over the top" />
  <figcaption>Canadian troops preparing to go over the top</figcaption>
</figure>

- Of the 801 soldiers, 700 became <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term> and only 68 answered roll call the next day to report for action

<figure>
  <img src="/g10-curriculum/Somme2.png" alt="Beaumont Hamel where the Newfoundland Regiment fell" />

</figure>

- A staggering 1.2 million <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term> occurred during this battle, 623,907 Allied <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term> and 660,000 German <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term>

<figure>
  <img src="/g10-curriculum/Somme1.png" alt="Massive pile of empty artillery shell casings" />
  <figcaption>The industrial scale of artillery bombardment at the Somme, leaving mountains of spent shell casings.</figcaption>
</figure>

- Over four months and with hundreds of thousands dead, the Allies only advanced 13km into the German line
- The battle was also notable for the first use of the <Term id="tank">tank</Term>, used to clear barbed wire and allow infantry to charge into enemy trenches

## Why is this important?

- In the Anglosphere, the words “The Somme” have become synonymous with the senseless slaughter and futile tactics of the First World War
- The phrase Lions led by Donkeys became associated with the battle, as gallant soldiers were led to their deaths by generals using outdated tactics that had not caught up with the scale of industrial, modern warfare

<figure>
  <img src="/g10-curriculum/Somme4.png" alt="Cavalry charge in World War I" />
  <figcaption>Traditional cavalry tactics proved tragically obsolete against modern barbed wire, artillery, and rapid-firing machine guns.</figcaption>
</figure>

- The effect of the Somme was catastrophic for the British Home Front, due to “<Term id="pals-battalions">Pals Battalions</Term>” when whole towns or regions saw a generation of their young men wiped out in a single day
- The shocking way the battle was conducted would lead a drive by British and Canadian soldiers to innovate based on the lessons learned with blood and lives
