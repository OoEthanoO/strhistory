## What happened?

- In February 1916, the German army launched a strategy of <Term id="attrition">attrition</Term> against the French Army at Verdun, with the goal, its commander later claimed, of bleeding France white
- This strategy of <Term id="attrition">attrition</Term> made the Battle of Verdun a slaughterhouse in which the French army was almost broken
- In order to relieve pressure on their French allies, the British, under their controversial commander Douglas Haig, took the lead in a planned Anglo-French offensive to draw German forces away from Verdun
- This offensive took place at a region called the Somme, named after the Somme river which flowed through the battlefield
- For months, Allied troops, mostly British and Dominion soldiers alongside the French, launched a series of catastrophic offensives against German positions on higher, more defensible ground

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/soldiers-muddy-road.jpg" alt="Soldiers in steel helmets and fleece coats marching along a muddy road beside pack horses">Soldiers in steel helmets and fleece coats march along a muddy road beside pack horses.</Figure>

- German soldiers called the battle *das Blutbad*, “the bloodbath”, in which whole <Term id="battalion">battalions</Term> were wiped out within minutes of action on the front
- On July 1st 1916, the first day of the offensive, 801 soldiers of the Newfoundland Regiment went <Term id="over-the-top">over the top</Term> to attack German trenches at Beaumont-Hamel
  - Newfoundland was then a separate Dominion; it did not join Canada until 1949

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/soldiers-fixed-bayonets.jpg" alt="Soldiers in steel helmets with fixed bayonets crowded in a trench, ready to climb out">Soldiers with fixed bayonets and gas-mask bags wait in a trench, ready to go over the top.</Figure>

- Of the 801 soldiers, about 700 became <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term> and only 68 answered roll call the next day to report for action
- From September to November 1916, the Canadian Corps fought at Courcelette, Thiepval Ridge and Regina Trench, suffering more than 24,000 <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term>

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/shattered-wood.jpg" alt="Landscape of broken tree trunks and churned-up ground">A wood shattered by shellfire on the Western Front.</Figure>

- More than a million soldiers became <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term> during this battle: 623,907 British, Dominion and French, and an estimated 400,000 to 680,000 German (the German figures are disputed)

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/shell-case-dump.jpg" alt="Huge pile of empty artillery shell cases, with ammunition boxes in front">A dump of empty artillery shell cases, a sign of the industrial scale of First World War bombardments.</Figure>

- Over four months and with hundreds of thousands dead, the Allies advanced only about 10 km into the German lines
- The battle was also notable for the first use of the <Term id="tank">tank</Term>, on September 15th 1916 at Flers–Courcelette, used to clear barbed wire and allow infantry to charge into enemy trenches

## Why is this important?

- In the Anglosphere, the words “The Somme” have become synonymous with the senseless slaughter and futile tactics of the First World War
- The phrase “lions led by donkeys” became associated with the battle, expressing a popular view that gallant soldiers were led to their deaths by generals whose outdated tactics had not caught up with the scale of industrial, modern warfare; historians still debate how fair this view is

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/cavalry-charge.jpg" alt="Mounted soldiers charging with drawn swords across open ground">Cavalry charging with drawn swords. On the Western Front, cavalry waited for a breakthrough that barbed wire, artillery and machine guns rarely allowed.</Figure>

- The effect of the Somme was catastrophic for the British Home Front because of “<Term id="pals-battalions">Pals Battalions</Term>”: whole towns or regions lost many of their young men in a single day
- The shocking way the battle was conducted drove British and Canadian soldiers to innovate, using lessons learned with blood and lives
