## Overview

In 1916 the German army launched a strategy of <Term id="attrition">attrition</Term> at the Battle of Verdun, designed to "bleed France white" — to inflict casualties so severe that the French army would break. By the summer the French were nearly broken. To relieve the pressure on their allies, the British, under the controversial commander General Sir Douglas Haig, launched a massive offensive on the River Somme in northern France.

<Chronology title="Key dates">
  <Event date="Feb–Jun 1916">Battle of Verdun; Germany attempts to destroy the French army through attrition, bringing France near collapse.</Event>
  <Event date="1 Jul 1916">First day of the Somme; 57,470 British casualties — the bloodiest day in the history of the British Army. 801 soldiers of the Newfoundland Regiment attack at Beaumont-Hamel; 700 become casualties.</Event>
  <Event date="Jul–Nov 1916">Allied offensives continue across the Somme; tanks are used in battle for the first time (15 September).</Event>
  <Event date="Nov 1916">Battle ends. Allied forces have advanced approximately 13 km at a cost of over 1.2 million casualties on both sides.</Event>
</Chronology>

## The strategy and its failure

The Somme offensive was designed to draw German forces away from Verdun by forcing them to defend a second front. Allied — mostly British, Imperial and Canadian — troops launched a series of attacks against German positions that were better defended and on higher ground. General Haig had hoped that a week-long artillery bombardment before the infantry advance would destroy German defences. It did not. The Germans sheltered in deep dugouts and emerged when the barrage lifted.

On 1 July 1916, the first day of the offensive, Allied troops went <Term id="over-the-top">over the top</Term> into withering machine-gun fire. It was the bloodiest single day in the history of the British Army.

<Callout type="context">
The <Term id="newfoundland-regiment">Newfoundland Regiment</Term>, fighting before Newfoundland joined Canada (it did not become a province until 1949), attacked German trenches at Beaumont-Hamel. Of 801 soldiers who went over the top, 700 became casualties. Only 68 men answered roll call the following morning. Memorial Park at Beaumont-Hamel is now a site of Canadian and Newfoundland commemoration.
</Callout>

The German nickname for the battle was *Das Blutbad* — "the Bloodbath." In which whole <Term id="battalion">battalions</Term> were wiped out in minutes. Over four months, with hundreds of thousands dead, the Allies advanced only 13 km into the German line.

## Casualties and the Home Front

A staggering 1.2 million casualties occurred during the battle: 623,907 Allied and approximately 660,000 German. The effect on the British Home Front was catastrophic, largely because of the practice of <Term id="pals-battalions">Pals Battalions</Term> — grouping men from the same town, city or region in a single unit. When a Pals Battalion suffered heavy losses, an entire community could lose its young men in a single day.

<Callout type="warning">
Pals Battalions were a British practice only — they did not occur in the Canadian Expeditionary Force, which mixed recruits from across the country.
</Callout>

## The tank and the lessons of the Somme

The battle also marked the first use of the <Term id="tank">tank</Term> in warfare, deployed on 15 September 1916 to clear barbed wire and allow infantry to advance on enemy trenches. Though early tanks were unreliable, the concept was proven: armoured, tracked vehicles could survive machine-gun fire that cut down advancing infantry.

<Callout type="historiography">
The phrase <Q by="Unnamed German general" note="The attribution is contested — it may be apocryphal, and was popularised by historian Alan Clark's 1961 book">Lions led by donkeys</Q> became associated with the Somme, suggesting that gallant soldiers were sent to their deaths by generals using outdated tactics. Historians debate this: some argue Haig and his commanders were genuinely learning under fire and adapting to conditions none had faced before; others maintain the leadership failure was real and preventable.
</Callout>

The shocking conduct of the battle drove British and Canadian soldiers to innovate:

- **Artillery coordination:** The Somme showed the need for artillery that moved with the infantry, not just a preliminary bombardment. This led directly to the creeping barrage perfected at Vimy Ridge.
- **Tactical decentralisation:** Junior officers and NCOs were given more initiative — a lesson that transformed the approach to the 1917 offensives.
- **Tanks:** The concept was proven at the Somme and refined for future battles.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="John Keegan" work="The Face of Battle" year="1976" school="Military history / experience of battle">
Keegan's close analysis of the first day of the Somme emphasises the failure of the artillery plan and the disconnect between high command and the men on the ground. He portrays the disaster as a product of technological and tactical ignorance — commanders who did not understand what artillery could and could not achieve against deep German dugouts.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Gary Sheffield" work="The Somme" year="2003" school="Revisionist">
Sheffield argues against the 'Lions led by Donkeys' interpretation, contending that the Somme was a necessary battle that relieved Verdun, inflicted serious losses on Germany, and forced the British Army through a painful but essential learning process. In his view, condemning the commanders without understanding the context is anachronistic.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why did the Battle of the Somme (1916) produce such enormous casualties for such limited territorial gains?">
German forces were dug into elevated, well-fortified positions. A week-long British artillery bombardment failed to destroy the deep German dugouts. When Allied troops went over the top on 1 July 1916, German machine-gunners emerged from their shelters and cut down the advancing infantry. Poor communication, rigid timetables, and inadequate tactics for breaking through prepared defences all contributed. Over the entire battle, 1.2 million casualties were sustained for an advance of only 13 km.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What military innovations emerged from — or were first used at — the Battle of the Somme?">
The tank was used in battle for the first time on 15 September 1916. The battle also demonstrated the limitations of purely preliminary artillery bombardment and drove the development of the creeping barrage (artillery moving just ahead of the infantry), which was perfected at Vimy Ridge in 1917. Junior officers were increasingly given tactical flexibility, reducing dependence on rigid top-down orders.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Is 'Lions led by Donkeys' a fair description of Allied leadership at the Somme?">
It is a debated judgement. Critics like Alan Clark point to the first day's catastrophic casualties as evidence of incompetent generalship. Revisionists like Gary Sheffield argue the commanders faced genuinely unprecedented conditions, that the battle served strategic purposes by relieving Verdun, and that the British Army visibly learned and improved during the battle. A balanced answer acknowledges both the serious failures of planning and execution and the broader strategic context.
</Recall>
