## Overview

On 22 April 1915 Allied troops were defending the town of Ypres in Belgium — a key transit point for supplies and troops arriving from Britain. The defensive line had created a <Term id="salient">salient</Term> around the city, placing Allied forces in a militarily vulnerable position. At 17:00, German forces opened 5,730 canisters releasing 160 tonnes of chlorine gas against Allied positions — the first large-scale use of chemical weapons in the war and a violation of the laws of war. When French and Algerian forces were forced to retreat 6 km, a gap opened in the line. Into that gap stepped the Canadians.

## The gas attack and Canada's response

The chlorine gas attacked soldiers' respiratory systems, forming hydrochloric acid in the lungs and effectively drowning them. The collapse of the Allied line to the north and east of Ypres threatened to unravel the entire salient.

<Callout type="context">
The <Term id="war-crime">war crime</Term> of deploying chemical weapons was prohibited under the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, which Germany had signed. The chlorine gas attack at Ypres was a deliberate violation intended to achieve a decisive breakthrough — one that the defenders' courage denied it.
</Callout>

Faced with this catastrophic situation, Canadian troops filled in the gap heroically, fending off the Germans for three days and even counterattacking German positions. When a second gas attack came, Canadian army medics improvised protection by recommending urine-soaked cloths held over the mouth and nose — the ammonia in urine neutralised some of the chlorine. Many Canadian soldiers suffered lifelong respiratory difficulties as a result of their exposure.

The human cost was severe: 6,036 Canadian casualties among roughly 60,000 British and Imperial casualties, against an estimated 34,873 German casualties.

## "In Flanders Fields"

After the battle, Canadian Lieutenant-Colonel John McCrae — a physician who had treated the wounded under fire — composed one of the most celebrated poems of the war:

The poem became an icon of Remembrance Day, commemorating the heroic sacrifice of Canadian soldiers at Ypres and, later, in battles across the world.

## Significance

The battle was a turning point for Canada's military reputation. Before Ypres, Canadian troops — mostly untrained volunteers — had been considered untested and secondary in quality to experienced British regulars. Their performance changed this assessment decisively.

- **Military credibility.** By defeating a world-class European army within twelve months of enlisting, Canadian troops proved their fighting quality to the Allied command.
- **Greater autonomy.** Ypres began a series of engagements in which the equal or superior performance of Canadian troops won them greater operational autonomy within the British Army structure.
- **National pride.** The battle proclaimed Canada's strength to the world for the first time, establishing a tradition of military excellence that would continue at Vimy Ridge and beyond.
- **Cultural legacy.** "In Flanders Fields" became inseparable from Canadian and Commonwealth commemoration culture, transforming the poppy into a universal symbol of remembrance.

When asked about the significance of Ypres, connect the military performance to the broader question of Canadian sovereignty and identity. The battle demonstrates how battlefield credibility translated into political demands for greater autonomy.
</Callout>

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Desmond Morton" work="When Your Number's Up: The Canadian Soldier in the First World War" year="1993" school="Social/military history">
Morton emphasises the improvised nature of Canadian preparation and the genuine heroism of ordinary soldiers at Ypres, arguing that the battle's importance lay not in sophisticated tactics — which were largely absent — but in the determination and adaptability of individual Canadians under extreme duress.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Tim Cook" work="At the Sharp End: Canadians Fighting the Great War 1914–1916" year="2007" school="Military history">
Cook argues that Ypres established a foundational myth of Canadian military identity: the citizen-soldier who rises to extraordinary challenges without the traditions or rigid hierarchy of professional armies. This myth, Cook contends, shaped how Canadians understood and remembered the war for a generation.
</Perspective>
