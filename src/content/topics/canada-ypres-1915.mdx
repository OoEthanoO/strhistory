## What happened?

- On April 22nd 1915, Allied troops were defending the Belgian town of Ypres, which stood between the German army and the Channel ports that brought troops and supplies from the UK
- Holding this <Term id="line">line</Term> had left a <Term id="salient">salient</Term> around the city of Ypres, a challenging military situation for the Allies
- Late that afternoon, the Germans released more than 160 tonnes of chlorine gas from 5,730 cylinders against Allied positions, in breach of the Hague Conventions’ ban on poison weapons, which made it a <Term id="war-crime">war crime</Term>
- The chlorine gas formed hydrochloric acid in the Allied soldiers’ lungs, drowning their lungs in fluid, and forced French and Algerian troops to retreat, leaving a gap about 6 km wide in the line
- Faced with the collapse of the <Term id="line">line</Term>, Canadian troops filled in the gap and heroically fended off the Germans for 3 days, even counterattacking the Germans

- Faced with further gas attacks, Canadian army medics recommended urine-soaked cloths as temporary gas masks, but many Canadian soldiers would face lifelong respiratory troubles from the chlorine gas

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/soldiers-box-respirators.jpg" alt="Soldiers in steel helmets and gas masks climbing out of a trench through clouds of gas or smoke">Soldiers in steel helmets and box respirators climb out of a trench through gas or smoke. This equipment came later in the war: at Ypres in April 1915, the Canadians had neither.</Figure>

- About 6,000 Canadian <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term> amongst about 60,000 British Empire <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term>, against about 35,000 German <Term id="casualty">casualties</Term>

<Figure src="/g10-curriculum/gassed-sargent-detail.jpg" alt="Painting of soldiers with bandaged eyes walking in a line, each holding the shoulder of the man in front, past wounded men lying on the ground">Detail of John Singer Sargent’s *Gassed* (1919), Imperial War Museum, London. Sargent painted soldiers blinded by mustard gas near Arras in August 1918: gas remained a weapon for the rest of the war.</Figure>

- During the battle, on May 3rd 1915, the Canadian doctor John McCrae wrote the famous poem “In <Term id="flanders-fields">Flanders Fields</Term>”, after the death of his friend Lieutenant Alexis Helmer

## Why is this important?

- The stand of the Canadian troops, previously considered untested and secondary in quality to British troops, was a resounding testament to their gallantry
- It was the beginning of a series of engagements in which the equal or superior performance of Canadian troops would earn them greater autonomy within the British army
- It proclaimed to the world for the first time the strength of Canada: in less than a year, untested volunteers had become soldiers who could hold their ground against the most powerful army in Europe
- “In Flanders Fields” would become an icon of Remembrance Day, commemorating first the sacrifice of Canadian soldiers at Ypres and later those who fell in battles across the world
