## Overview

Between 1933 and 1940 Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy broke the peace settlement of 1919 step by step. The League of Nations failed to stop Italy in Abyssinia; Britain and France followed a policy of <Term id="appeasement">appeasement</Term> towards Germany until March 1939. When Hitler invaded Poland, they declared war — and by June 1940 France had fallen and Italy had joined the war.

## Aims

**Hitler** wanted to overturn Versailles, unite all Germans in one Reich, and win <Term id="lebensraum">Lebensraum</Term> ("living space") in the East at the expense of Poland and the USSR — aims set out in *Mein Kampf* and bound up with his racial ideology.

**Mussolini** wanted a new Roman Empire: dominance of the Mediterranean and colonies in Africa, avenging Italy's defeat at Adwa in 1896 and its disappointment at the peace settlement.

## Abyssinia and the failure of collective security

Italy's invasion of Abyssinia in October 1935 was the League's great test in Europe. The League imposed sanctions but excluded oil, and Britain did not close the Suez Canal. The Hoare–Laval Pact, which would have given Italy much of Abyssinia, caused outrage when leaked. Italy completed its conquest in May 1936 — and drifted towards Germany.

## Appeasement

<Callout type="context" title="Why appease?">
British and French leaders had strong reasons: memories of the First World War, economic weakness, the need to rearm first, sympathy for some German grievances about Versailles, fear of communism, doubts about Dominion and American support, and fear of bombing. French strategy was defensive, built around the Maginot Line.
</Callout>

The policy peaked in the Sudeten crisis of September 1938. As war seemed close, Chamberlain told the British people on the radio:

<KeyQuote by="Neville Chamberlain" role="British Prime Minister" date="27 September 1938" source="Radio broadcast" analysis="Chamberlain's words reflect a widespread reluctance to fight for Czechoslovakia — and ignore the Czechs' own position. They are often used to show how appeasement treated smaller states as bargaining chips.">How horrible, fantastic, incredible it is that we should be digging trenches and trying on gas-masks here because of a quarrel in a far away country between people of whom we know nothing.</KeyQuote>

At Munich, Britain, France, Germany and Italy agreed that Germany would occupy the Sudetenland; Czechoslovakia was not consulted. Chamberlain returned claiming <Q by="Neville Chamberlain" date="30 September 1938" source="Speech outside 10 Downing Street" note="The phrase became a byword for the failure of appeasement when Hitler occupied Prague six months later. In fairness, Chamberlain also accelerated British rearmament.">peace for our time</Q>. Churchill told the Commons: <Q by="Winston Churchill" date="5 October 1938" source="Speech in the House of Commons" note="Churchill was isolated in 1938; his warnings gave him credibility when appeasement collapsed.">We have sustained a total and unmitigated defeat</Q>.

## From Prague to Poland

The occupation of Prague in March 1939 showed that Hitler's aims went beyond uniting Germans. Britain and France guaranteed Poland. The Nazi–Soviet Pact of August 1939, with its secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe, removed the threat of a two-front war, and Hitler invaded Poland on 1 September. Mussolini, unready for a long war despite the Pact of Steel, stayed out until France was collapsing in June 1940.

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="A. J. P. Taylor" work="The Origins of the Second World War" year="1961" school="Hitler as opportunist">
Taylor argued that Hitler was a traditional German statesman who exploited opportunities others gave him rather than following a blueprint — a deliberately provocative thesis.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Hugh Trevor-Roper" work="'A. J. P. Taylor, Hitler and the War'" year="1961" school="Intentionalist">
Trevor-Roper replied that Hitler pursued consistent long-term aims, above all Lebensraum in the East, set out in Mein Kampf.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="John Charmley" work="Chamberlain and the Lost Peace" year="1989" school="Revisionist on appeasement">
Charmley defended Chamberlain's policy as a rational response to Britain's military, economic and imperial weaknesses, against the "Guilty Men" view that appeasers were naïve or cowardly.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why was the remilitarisation of the Rhineland significant?">
It broke Versailles and Locarno, and Britain and France did nothing. It strengthened Hitler's position at home and abroad, allowed Germany to fortify its western border, and made France's Eastern European alliances harder to honour.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Give four reasons Britain followed a policy of appeasement.">
Fear of another world war and of bombing; the need to rearm first; economic weakness after the Depression; sympathy for German grievances over Versailles; fear of communism; and uncertain support from the Dominions and the USA.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why did the Nazi–Soviet Pact make war over Poland likely?">
It removed the threat of Soviet intervention and a two-front war, so Hitler believed he could invade Poland — and that Britain and France, unable to help Poland directly, might back down.
</Recall>
