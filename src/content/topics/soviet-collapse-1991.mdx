## Overview

A failed coup against Mikhail Gorbachev in August 1991 accelerated the collapse of Soviet authority. Boris Yeltsin emerged in a stronger position, while the relationship between the union and its republics fractured.

<Chronology>
  <Event date="August 1991">The attempted coup against Gorbachev failed.</Event>
  <Event date="December 1991">The Soviet Union dissolved.</Event>
</Chronology>

The [US Office of the Historian's account](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1989-1992/collapse-soviet-union) traces the political sequence and the American government's response.

## A crisis of authority

The coup's failure damaged those trying to preserve the old order and weakened Gorbachev's position. The formation of the <Term id="commonwealth-independent-states">Commonwealth of Independent States</Term> in December signalled the replacement of union authority by relations between independent states.

This was not simply a change of leader within an otherwise unchanged government. An inquiry into transition must identify which institutions disappeared, which survived and where authority moved.

## Interpreting the evidence

The Office of the Historian's account concentrates on US diplomacy, security and responses to the crisis. That focus makes it useful for studying international reactions. It should be supplemented by evidence from people and institutions within the former Soviet republics when investigating their experiences.

Political dissolution did not by itself resolve questions about prices, ownership, employment or living standards. Claims about the outcomes of economic reform require evidence extending beyond the ceremony or declaration that marked a political transition.
