## Overview

In the 1950s African Americans in the South lived under <Term id="jim-crow">Jim Crow</Term> segregation and were largely denied the vote. Between the Supreme Court's ruling in <Term id="brown-v-board">Brown v. Board of Education</Term> (1954) and the <Term id="voting-rights-act-1965">Voting Rights Act</Term> (1965), a mass movement forced the end of legal segregation. This prescribed subject asks how the movement did it — and who deserves the credit.

## Discrimination in the 1950s

Segregation laws separated Black and white Americans in schools, transport, restaurants and public places across the South, upheld since *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) by the doctrine of "separate but equal". Poll taxes, literacy tests and intimidation kept most Southern Black citizens from voting. Violence, from lynching to bombings, enforced the system. Outside the South, discrimination in housing and employment was widespread too.

## The legal challenge

The NAACP's lawyers, led by Thurgood Marshall, attacked segregation in the courts.

<KeyQuote by="Chief Justice Earl Warren" role="for a unanimous Supreme Court" date="17 May 1954" source="Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka" analysis="The unanimous ruling overturned the legal basis of segregation. But the Court's 1955 order to desegregate 'with all deliberate speed' allowed years of Southern 'massive resistance' — showing the limits of change through the courts alone.">In the field of public education the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.</KeyQuote>

## Mass protest and nonviolent direct action

The Montgomery bus boycott, sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest in December 1955, lasted 381 days and brought Martin Luther King Jr. to national prominence. King and the <Term id="sclc">SCLC</Term> developed a strategy of <Term id="nonviolent-direct-action">nonviolent direct action</Term>: peaceful protests that exposed the violence of segregation to the nation and the world.

Students took the lead with the sit-ins of 1960 and the Freedom Rides of 1961. In Birmingham in 1963, televised images of police using dogs and fire hoses on young marchers shocked the country. Jailed there, King answered white clergy who urged patience:

<KeyQuote by="Martin Luther King Jr." role="President of the SCLC" date="16 April 1963" source="Letter from Birmingham Jail" analysis="King defended direct action against those who called it 'untimely'. The letter linked local protest to universal principles of justice — a key text for evaluating the movement's methods and aims.">Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere.</KeyQuote>

At the March on Washington, King told a crowd of about 250,000 that he had <Q by="Martin Luther King Jr." date="28 August 1963" source="Speech at the March on Washington" note="The speech placed the movement within American ideals — the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution — helping build the national coalition that passed the Civil Rights Act.">a dream that my four little children will one day live in a nation where they will not be judged by the color of their skin but by the content of their character</Q>.

## The federal government

Federal action was uneven. Eisenhower sent troops to Little Rock to enforce a court order, but reluctantly. Kennedy moved cautiously until Birmingham convinced him to propose a civil rights bill. After Kennedy's assassination, Lyndon Johnson used his legislative skill to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964; the violence at Selma led to the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

<Callout type="historiography" title="Different perspectives">
Other voices challenged King's approach. <Term id="malcolm-x">Malcolm X</Term> criticised nonviolence and integration as goals, and SNCC activists increasingly questioned the strategy by 1965. Remember too that local organisers — often women such as Ella Baker and Fannie Lou Hamer — did much of the movement's work.
</Callout>

## Historians' debate

<Perspective historian="Taylor Branch" work="Parting the Waters" year="1988" school="Leadership">
Branch's biography-based narrative put King at the centre of the movement's story, connecting his leadership to national politics.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Charles M. Payne" work="I've Got the Light of Freedom" year="1995" school="Grassroots">
Payne argued that the movement's real strength lay in patient local organising in places like Mississippi, often led by ordinary people and women rather than national figures.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Mary L. Dudziak" work="Cold War Civil Rights" year="2000" school="International context">
Dudziak showed how segregation damaged America's image in the Cold War, giving the federal government a foreign-policy reason to support civil rights reform.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="Why was the Brown decision important — and what were its limits?">
It declared school segregation unconstitutional, destroying the legal basis of "separate but equal". But the order to desegregate "with all deliberate speed" allowed Southern states to resist for years; very few Southern Black children attended integrated schools by 1964.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why was nonviolence an effective strategy?">
It gave the movement the moral high ground, attracted national media attention, and exposed the violence of segregationists — as at Birmingham and Selma — which built public support and pushed the federal government to act.
</Recall>

<Recall q="What did the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) do?">
The Civil Rights Act outlawed segregation in public places and discrimination in employment. The Voting Rights Act banned literacy tests and gave the federal government power to oversee voter registration in states with a record of discrimination.
</Recall>
