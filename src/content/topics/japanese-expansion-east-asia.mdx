## Overview

In September 1931 officers of Japan's <Term id="kwantung-army">Kwantung Army</Term> staged an explosion on a Japanese-owned railway near Mukden and used it as a pretext to conquer Manchuria. Over the next decade Japan left the <Term id="league-of-nations">League of Nations</Term>, fought an undeclared war with China from 1937, and in December 1941 attacked the United States and the British and Dutch empires in Southeast Asia. This case study asks why expansion happened, why no one stopped it, and why it escalated into a Pacific war.

## Why Japan expanded

Japan had been building an empire since its victories over China (1894–95) and Russia (1904–05), which gave it Taiwan, Korea and rights in southern Manchuria. Three pressures pushed it further in the 1930s.

**Economic crisis.** The Great Depression devastated Japan's export trade — especially raw silk to the United States — and rural poverty deepened. Manchuria's coal, iron and farmland looked like a solution: a secure source of raw materials and a market that Japan controlled.

**Nationalism and militarism.** Many army officers, nationalist societies and much of the press saw party politicians as corrupt and weak, and the 1920s agreements with the West — above all the <Term id="washington-naval-conference">Washington Naval Conference</Term> — as humiliating. Political violence became routine: Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi was assassinated by naval officers in May 1932, and army radicals attempted a coup in February 1936.

**Strategic fear.** Army planners saw Manchuria as a buffer against the Soviet Union and watched Chinese nationalism — Chiang Kai-shek's campaign to reunify China — threaten Japan's position there.

<Callout type="context" title="Who made policy?">
Expansion was often driven from below. The Kwantung Army acted in 1931 without the cabinet's approval, and Tokyo then accepted the result. Historians call this pattern <Term id="gekokujo">gekokujō</Term> — "the lower overturning the higher".
</Callout>

## Manchuria, 1931–1933

After the Mukden explosion, the Kwantung Army overran Manchuria within months, despite the civilian government's attempts to limit the fighting. In 1932 Japan created Manchukuo, a nominally independent state that it controlled completely.

China appealed to the League, which sent a commission of inquiry under Lord Lytton. Its report (October 1932) rejected Japan's claim that it had acted in self-defence and recommended that Manchuria be returned to Chinese sovereignty with wide autonomy — but proposed no sanctions.

<KeyQuote by="Lytton Commission" role="League of Nations commission of inquiry" date="October 1932" source="Report of the Commission of Enquiry" analysis="The League's own investigators rejected Japan's justification for the invasion — yet the League imposed no sanctions. The gap between judgement and action is the heart of any answer about the League's failure.">The military operations of the Japanese troops during this night … cannot be regarded as measures of legitimate self-defence.</KeyQuote>

When the League Assembly adopted the report in February 1933, the Japanese delegation walked out, and Japan announced its withdrawal from the League in March. No member was willing to impose economic sanctions or send forces: Britain and France had interests of their own in Asia and were preoccupied by the Depression, and the United States — the one power with real leverage in the Pacific — was not a member.

## From Manchuria to war with China, 1933–1937

A truce in 1933 left Japan in control of Manchuria and the neighbouring province of Jehol, and Japanese pressure on northern China continued. At home the army's influence grew after the failed coup of February 1936, and Japan drew closer to Nazi Germany through the Anti-Comintern Pact.

On 7 July 1937 a skirmish at the Marco Polo Bridge near Beijing escalated into all-out war. Japan expected a quick victory; instead Chinese resistance, the United Front between the Guomindang and the Communists, and China's sheer size produced a long war of attrition. The capture of Nanjing in December 1937 was followed by weeks of mass killing and rape of civilians and prisoners.

<KeyQuote by="Prince Konoe Fumimaro" role="Prime Minister of Japan" date="3 November 1938" source="Statement on the New Order in East Asia" analysis="Konoe recast an unwinnable war as a crusade for a Japan-led Asia free of Western influence. The phrase became the basis of the later Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere — and signalled that Japan's aims now extended beyond China.">What Japan seeks is the establishment of a new order which will ensure the permanent stability of East Asia.</KeyQuote>

## The road to Pearl Harbor, 1939–1941

By 1939 Japan was bogged down in China and had been defeated by Soviet forces in border fighting at Nomonhan (Khalkhin Gol). German victories in Europe in 1940 changed the calculation: the colonies of defeated France and the Netherlands, and a hard-pressed Britain, left Southeast Asia's oil, rubber and tin within reach. Japan announced the <Term id="co-prosperity-sphere">Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere</Term>, moved into northern Indochina, and signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy. A neutrality pact with the USSR in April 1941 secured Japan's northern flank.

The United States responded with escalating economic pressure. When Japan occupied southern Indochina in July 1941, Washington froze Japanese assets and cut off oil; Britain and the Netherlands followed. Japan imported most of its oil from the United States, so its leaders faced a choice between withdrawing from China and Indochina or seizing the oil of the Dutch East Indies by force. Negotiations failed, and on 7 December 1941 Japan struck.

<KeyQuote by="Franklin D. Roosevelt" role="President of the United States" date="8 December 1941" source="Address to Congress" analysis="Roosevelt's framing — a surprise attack during negotiations — unified American opinion behind war. For the prescribed subject, note that the attack was the culmination of a decade of escalation, not a bolt from the blue.">Yesterday, December 7th, 1941 — a date which will live in infamy — the United States of America was suddenly and deliberately attacked by naval and air forces of the Empire of Japan.</KeyQuote>

## How the world responded

| Response | When | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| League of Nations: Lytton Commission | 1932–33 | Condemned Japan's actions; no sanctions; Japan left the League |
| United States: Stimson Doctrine | January 1932 | Refused to recognise conquests made in breach of the Kellogg–Briand Pact, but took no action |
| Brussels Conference | November 1937 | Called to address the China war; ended without agreement on any measures |
| US economic pressure | 1939–41 | Commercial treaty ended, then embargoes on scrap iron, aviation fuel and finally oil |

## Historians' debate

Why did Japan choose expansion? Historians weigh planning against improvisation, and ideology against security.

<Perspective historian="International Military Tribunal for the Far East" work="Judgment" year="1948" school="Conspiracy thesis">
The Tokyo war crimes tribunal concluded that a group of militarists had conspired from the late 1920s to secure Japanese domination of East Asia. Later historians have found this too tidy: policy was contested and often improvised.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="James B. Crowley" work="Japan's Quest for Autonomy" year="1966" school="Security and autonomy">
Crowley argued that Japanese leaders pursued a rational — if ultimately disastrous — search for national security and economic self-sufficiency in a hostile international environment.
</Perspective>

<Perspective historian="Herbert P. Bix" work="Hirohito and the Making of Modern Japan" year="2000" school="Role of the emperor">
Bix challenged the post-war image of Emperor Hirohito as a powerless figurehead, arguing that he was actively involved in decisions for war.
</Perspective>

## Test yourself

<Recall q="What was the Mukden Incident, and why does it matter?">
On 18 September 1931 Kwantung Army officers blew up a section of the Japanese-owned South Manchuria Railway and blamed Chinese troops, using it as a pretext to occupy Manchuria. It matters because the army acted without government approval and the government accepted the result — a sign that the military was driving policy.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Give two reasons why the League of Nations did not stop Japan in Manchuria.">
It had no armed force of its own and its leading members, Britain and France, were unwilling to impose sanctions or risk war in Asia during the Depression. The United States, the power with most influence over Japan, was not a member. The Lytton inquiry also took over a year, by which time Manchukuo was a fait accompli.
</Recall>

<Recall q="Why was the US oil embargo of 1941 a turning point?">
Japan depended on imported oil, most of it from the United States. The embargo left it with a stark choice: give up its gains in China and Indochina, or seize the oil of the Dutch East Indies — which meant war with the United States and the British Empire.
</Recall>

<Recall q="How did events in Europe in 1940 encourage Japanese expansion?">
Germany's defeat of France and the Netherlands left their Asian colonies weakly defended, and Britain was fighting for survival. This opened Southeast Asia's resources to Japan and led to the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy.
</Recall>
